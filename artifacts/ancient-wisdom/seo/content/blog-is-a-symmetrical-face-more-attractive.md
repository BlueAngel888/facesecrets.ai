## The short answer

Experiments that show people original faces next to digitally symmetrised versions usually find a modest preference for the more symmetrical version (for example Rhodes and colleagues, 1998; Perrett and colleagues, 1999). The effect is real but small, and it varies between faces and observers.

## Why symmetry might matter

One theory is that symmetry signals developmental stability: a body that grew without much disruption. Evidence that facial symmetry predicts actual health is mixed and weak, so the preference may simply reflect how the visual system processes faces; symmetrical patterns are easier to process.

## Why perfect symmetry looks odd

Mirror composites made from two left halves or two right halves often look unfamiliar, slightly artificial, or like a different person. Real faces are always a little asymmetrical, and natural expressions, especially smiles, are rarely identical on both sides.

## What matters more

| Factor | What research suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Averageness | Faces close to the population average are rated attractive (Langlois and Roggman, 1990) |
| Expression | A genuine smile raises attractiveness ratings more than small changes in symmetry |
| Skin | Even skin tone and texture strongly affect ratings |
| Familiarity | People tend to like faces they have seen before, including their own mirror image |
| Context | Grooming, hair, lighting and camera distance change perception a lot |
