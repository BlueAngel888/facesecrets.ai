## What are microexpressions?

Microexpressions are very short expressions of emotion, usually lasting less than half a second. They were first described by Haggard and Isaacs in 1966 as "micromomentary" expressions, and studied extensively by psychologists Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen from the late 1960s.

They differ from ordinary expressions in two ways: they are **fast**, and they are often **partial or suppressed**, appearing when a person tries to mask a feeling.

## The 7 universal emotions

Ekman's cross-cultural research proposed that some facial expressions of emotion are recognised around the world. The commonly cited list has seven:

| Emotion | Key facial signs | Main action units (FACS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Happiness | Cheeks raised, crow's feet, lip corners pulled up | AU6 + AU12 |
| Sadness | Inner brows raised, lip corners pulled down | AU1 + AU4 + AU15 |
| Surprise | Brows raised, eyes wide, jaw dropped | AU1 + AU2 + AU5 + AU26 |
| Fear | Brows raised and drawn together, upper lids raised, lips stretched | AU1 + AU2 + AU4 + AU5 + AU20 |
| Anger | Brows lowered and drawn together, lids tightened, lips pressed | AU4 + AU5 + AU7 + AU23 |
| Disgust | Nose wrinkled, upper lip raised | AU9 + AU10 |
| Contempt | One lip corner tightened and raised | Unilateral AU14 |

Contempt is the only one that is typically one-sided, which makes it a good example of how asymmetry carries meaning in expressions. Debate continues about how universal these categories are; later research shows culture and context shape both how emotions are shown and how they are read.

## The Facial Action Coding System (FACS)

FACS, published by Ekman and Friesen in 1978 and revised in 2002, breaks every visible facial movement into **action units** (AUs), each tied to one or more muscles. AU12, for example, is the zygomatic major pulling the lip corners up. Trained coders use FACS to describe expressions objectively, without guessing what the person feels. It is used in psychology, animation and affective computing.

## How to recognise microexpressions

1. **Know the baseline.** Watch how a person's face looks at rest and in neutral conversation.
2. **Look at the upper face.** The brows and eyelids are harder to control than the mouth.
3. **Watch for mismatches.** A smile without the eyes, or a flash of a frown during praise.
4. **Use slow motion.** Video at reduced speed makes 200-millisecond expressions visible.
5. **Consider context.** An expression shows an emotion, not its cause.

## Can microexpressions detect lies?

Not reliably. Microexpressions can show that someone feels an emotion they are not expressing, but not why. Studies of lie detection find that most people, including many professionals, perform close to chance. A 2013 US Government Accountability Office review criticised an airport screening programme based on behavioural cues for lacking scientific validation. Use microexpressions to become more attentive to feelings, not to accuse.

## Microexpressions in a still photo

A single photo cannot show a microexpression, because microexpressions are defined by their timing. A photo can show the **resting expression** and lines formed by habitual expressions, such as smile lines or vertical lines between the brows. That is what a FaceSecrets report describes, alongside the modern FACS/Ekman perspective.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a microexpression last?

Usually between about 1/25 of a second and half a second.

### Can you train yourself to read microexpressions?

Training tools exist and can improve recognition of expressions in tests. Whether that improves real-world judgement is less clear.

### What is the difference between a real and a fake smile?

A genuine (Duchenne) smile involves the muscles around the eyes as well as the mouth. Read [real vs fake smile](/blog/real-vs-fake-smile-duchenne).
