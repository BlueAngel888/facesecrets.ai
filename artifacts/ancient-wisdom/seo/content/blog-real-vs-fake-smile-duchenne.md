## Where the Duchenne smile comes from

In 1862 the French neurologist Guillaume Duchenne de Boulogne used electrical stimulation to study facial muscles. He noticed that a smile of real enjoyment engages the muscle around the eye, the *orbicularis oculi*, as well as the *zygomatic major* that lifts the lip corners. Paul Ekman later named the enjoyment smile after him.

## The signs of a Duchenne smile

| Sign | Duchenne (enjoyment) smile | Polite or posed smile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lip corners | Pulled up and back (AU12) | Pulled up (AU12) |
| Cheeks | Raised, pushing up the lower eyelid (AU6) | Little or no cheek raise |
| Eyes | Narrowed, crow's feet at the outer corners | Eyes largely unchanged |
| Timing | Smooth onset and fade | Often switched on and off abruptly |
| Symmetry | Usually fairly symmetrical | Can be lopsided |

## Why it is not that simple

Later research found that many people can produce cheek raising on purpose, and that a broad smile raises the cheeks anyway. So crow's feet are a hint, not proof. Timing and context are often more informative than any single feature.

## Smiles in photos

Photos capture one moment, so posed smiles dominate. If you want a natural-looking photo, think of something that genuinely amuses you, or laugh and let the photographer catch the fade: the eyes join in.

## Smile lines and face reading

Lines at the corners of the eyes and the folds beside the mouth show which expressions a face makes often. In face-reading traditions, these "laughter lines" are read as warmth and sociability. They are one of the few traditional readings that connect directly to observable behaviour. Learn more in our [microexpressions guide](/guides/microexpressions).
