## What is facial symmetry?

Facial symmetry is how closely the left half of a face mirrors the right half across a vertical midline. Scientists distinguish two kinds of asymmetry:

- **Directional asymmetry**: a consistent difference found across most people, such as one side of the face being slightly larger on average.
- **Fluctuating asymmetry**: small, random deviations that differ from person to person. This is the kind most attractiveness research studies.

Everyday factors also create asymmetry: sleeping on one side, chewing habits, dental work, injuries, and above all expression. A half-smile or a raised eyebrow makes any face asymmetrical for a moment.

## How symmetry is measured

| Method | How it works | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Landmark distances | Mark points (eye corners, mouth corners, nostrils) and compare their distances from the midline | Research, orthodontics, surgery planning |
| Mirror composites | Build "left-left" and "right-right" faces from each half | Visual demonstrations and perception studies |
| 3D surface scans | Compare the two halves of a 3D model | Clinical and anthropological research |
| Pixel comparison | Compare a photo with its mirror image | Quick estimates, like our free test |

Photo conditions matter as much as the face. A head turned a few degrees, side lighting or a wide-angle phone lens close to the face can create more apparent asymmetry than the face really has.

## Are symmetrical faces more attractive?

Partly. Studies such as Rhodes and colleagues (1998) and Perrett and colleagues (1999) found that people rate more symmetrical versions of faces as somewhat more attractive. But the effect is modest, and other factors matter at least as much: averageness (faces close to the population average, as shown by Langlois and Roggman in 1990), skin quality, expression and familiarity.

Perfect symmetry is not the goal. Fully mirrored faces often look slightly unnatural. Read more in [is a symmetrical face more attractive?](/blog/is-a-symmetrical-face-more-attractive)

## Symmetry and the golden ratio

The golden ratio (about 1.618) is often claimed to define the ideal face. Evidence for that is weak. A 2010 study by Pallett, Link and Lee found faces were rated most attractive when the eye-to-mouth distance was about 36% of face length and the distance between the eyes was about 46% of face width; these "new golden ratios" correspond to an average face, not to 1.618. See [golden ratio face: myth or measurement?](/blog/golden-ratio-face)

## What symmetry does not tell you

Symmetry is not a measure of personality, intelligence, honesty or worth. Some studies have linked fluctuating asymmetry to developmental stability or health, but the links are small, inconsistent and cannot be read from one photo. In face-reading traditions, a balanced face is read as inner balance; treat that as a metaphor for reflection.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it normal for one eye to be bigger than the other?

Yes. Small differences in eye size, brow height and mouth corners are common. A sudden change in symmetry, such as one side of the face drooping, is different: it can be a medical emergency and needs a doctor immediately.

### Why does my face look different in selfies?

Front cameras usually show a mirrored image, the lens is close to your face and wide-angle distortion enlarges the nose and center of the face. You are used to your mirror image, so the unflipped photo can look "off".

### Can I make my face more symmetrical?

Most visible asymmetry comes from posture, expression and lighting. Even lighting, a straight head position and a relaxed face make photos look more balanced.
