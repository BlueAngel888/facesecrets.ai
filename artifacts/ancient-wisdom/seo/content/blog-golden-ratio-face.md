## What is the golden ratio?

The golden ratio, written φ (phi), is about 1.618. Two quantities are in the golden ratio when the larger divided by the smaller equals the sum divided by the larger. It appears in mathematics and in some natural growth patterns, and has been linked, often loosely, to art and architecture.

## The claim about faces

Popular sources say an ideal face has many proportions close to 1.618: face length to width, nose width to mouth width, and so on. Some cosmetic marketing uses "golden ratio masks" to score faces.

## What the evidence says

- Measurements of faces rated attractive do not cluster tightly around 1.618.
- Different calculators use different landmarks and ratios, so the same face can score very differently.
- A 2010 study by Pallett, Link and Lee tested which proportions people preferred. Faces looked best when the vertical distance between eyes and mouth was about **36%** of face length and the horizontal distance between the eyes was about **46%** of face width. These match the average face, which supports **averageness** rather than phi.

## Why the myth persists

The golden ratio is a tidy number with a long cultural history, and ratios are easy to calculate from a photo. That makes it attractive for quizzes and apps, even though attractiveness depends on expression, skin, averageness, familiarity and context.

## A more useful way to look at proportions

Face-reading traditions also care about proportion, but as balance between zones rather than one magic number. Chinese face reading, for example, compares the upper, middle and lower thirds of the face. See [the face reading guide](/guides/face-reading).
